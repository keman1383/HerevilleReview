# TOWN OF HEREVILLE, VIRGINIA
## Community History Brief: Development, Division, and the Bypass
**Document No.:** HVL-HIST-001  
**Prepared by:** Hereville Office of Community Development  
**Sources:** Virginia Department of Historic Resources; Hereville Municipal Archives; Blue Ridge Regional Library Local History Collection  
**Date:** March 2024  
**Distribution:** Public Record

---

### OVERVIEW

This brief was prepared as part of the Hereville Comprehensive Plan update process to provide historical context for current land use and demographic patterns. It is intended as a planning reference document, not an exhaustive historical account.

---

### FOUNDING AND EARLY DEVELOPMENT (1820s–1940s)

Hereville was established as a trading post community in the 1820s at the intersection of two regional wagon roads connecting the Alleghany Highlands to the Shenandoah Valley and points east. Its early economy centered on timber processing, small-scale agriculture, and the provisioning of travelers. A rail spur constructed in 1887 briefly accelerated growth, supporting a small furniture manufacturing operation and several commercial enterprises along what is now Main Street.

By the early twentieth century, Hereville had a stable if modest population of approximately 2,800. The town was racially segregated by law and custom. Black residents, comprising approximately 22% of the population at the 1930 census, were concentrated in the southern portion of town — an area informally known as the Garrison District, named for Garrison Street, its central thoroughfare. Deed restrictions and informal lending practices limited Black homeownership to this area through the mid-twentieth century.

The Garrison District developed a self-sufficient commercial and civic infrastructure during the segregation era, including several Black-owned businesses on Garrison Street, two churches (including New Bethel AME, established 1903), a fraternal lodge, and Garrison Street School, which served Black students from grades 1 through 11.

### URBAN RENEWAL AND SCHOOL DESEGREGATION (1950s–1970s)

**School Desegregation**

Hereville City Schools desegregated under court order in 1966, twelve years after *Brown v. Board of Education*. Garrison Street School was closed upon desegregation. Black students were reassigned to the previously all-white elementary school, which was located on the north side of Route 14. Garrison Street School's building was transferred to the town and subsequently used for municipal storage before being demolished in 1978.

No Black faculty from Garrison Street School were retained by Hereville City Schools following desegregation. School board minutes from 1966 reflect a determination that "existing staff vacancies are insufficient to absorb additional personnel at this time."

The closure of Garrison Street School was consistent with a pattern documented statewide: in the majority of Virginia localities that desegregated during this period, Black schools were closed rather than white schools, and Black educators were disproportionately displaced.

**Urban Renewal**

Between 1962 and 1971, the Town of Hereville participated in a federally funded urban renewal program targeting the Garrison District. Approximately 34 structures were condemned and demolished, displacing an estimated 180–220 residents. The stated rationale was substandard housing conditions. A planned replacement housing development was partially constructed on the eastern edge of the Garrison District; the remainder of the cleared land was never redeveloped and remains vacant or in low-intensity use.

Several Garrison Street commercial properties were among those demolished. The combination of school closure, displacement, and commercial demolition significantly diminished the economic and civic infrastructure of the Garrison District during this period.

### THE BYPASS AND ITS AFTERMATH (1980s–PRESENT)

**Construction of Route 220 Bypass**

In 1987, the Virginia Department of Transportation completed the Route 220 bypass, routing through traffic around Hereville's commercial center. The bypass was constructed primarily through undeveloped land north and northwest of town, consistent with the topography and land availability of that area.

The economic impact was immediate and sustained. Businesses dependent on through-traffic declined sharply. Between 1987 and 1995, Hereville lost an estimated 340 jobs in retail, food service, and hospitality — approximately 18% of total local employment at the time. Several Main Street businesses closed permanently. A regional grocery chain that had operated in Hereville since 1971 closed its location in 1992.

**Residential Development Following the Bypass**

The bypass created conditions for residential development on the north side of town, which had improved highway access to regional employment centers. Between 1988 and 2005, approximately 340 new housing units were constructed in Hereville, concentrated almost entirely in the north side. This development attracted younger families and households with higher incomes relative to the existing population.

The south side of town, which had less direct access to the bypass interchange and continued to carry the effects of urban renewal displacement, did not experience comparable residential investment. Housing stock in the Garrison District and surrounding areas continued to age without significant reinvestment.

The result, documented in successive census cycles, is a widening geographic divergence in household income, homeownership rates, and racial composition between the north and south sides of Hereville — a divergence that was not present to the same degree before 1987.

---

### SCHOOL ATTENDANCE ZONES AND THE BYPASS

Southside Elementary School was constructed in 1961 on Garrison Street, in part to serve the growing Black community in the surrounding neighborhood and in part to relieve overcrowding at the then-existing white elementary school. Its attendance zone has remained substantially unchanged since 1966.

Northside Elementary School was constructed in 1987 — the same year the bypass opened — to serve the new residential development on the north side of town. Its attendance zone was drawn to correspond to the new subdivisions.

The current attendance zone boundary follows Route 14, the same east-west corridor that has historically divided the north and south sides of Hereville. The boundary has not been formally reviewed or revised since Northside Elementary opened.

---

### CURRENT CONDITIONS

The south side of Hereville retains a higher concentration of Black and Latino residents, lower median household incomes, higher rates of rental occupancy, and lower homeownership rates than the north side (see HVL-EDU-003). These patterns are the cumulative result of historical segregation, urban renewal displacement, differential development following the bypass, and the absence of sustained reinvestment in the south side over several decades.

No finding in this document is intended to assign legal liability or to characterize any current resident, official, or institution as responsible for historical actions. The patterns described are presented as context for current planning decisions.
